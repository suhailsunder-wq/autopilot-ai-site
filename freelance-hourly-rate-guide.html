How to calculate your freelance hourly rate

When you work for yourself, your hourly rate has to cover more than your time. This guide helps you estimate a personal minimum rate based on the money you need, the costs of running your business and the hours you can realistically bill.

1. Start with your annual take-home goal

Choose how much money you want to keep after business taxes. Don't start by copying another freelancer's hourly rate: their living costs, taxes, experience and billable hours may be different. If your target is $4,000 per month after taxes, your annual take-home target is $48,000.

2. Add business expenses

Estimate the money your work costs each year: software subscriptions, professional insurance, equipment, hosting, training, payment fees and other legitimate work expenses. If you are new, use conservative estimates and update them as you get real invoices. For this example, we'll use $6,000 per year.

3. Account for your own taxes

In a simplified estimate, if you reserve 20% of business profit for tax, you need more than $48,000 in profit to keep $48,000. The example assumes a flat reserve percentage applied to profit, not a real tax calculation. Actual tax systems can use brackets, thresholds and deductions; speak with a qualified adviser for your circumstances.

4. Estimate billable hours — not just working hours

You may spend time writing proposals, marketing, learning, bookkeeping and talking with clients. Those hours are work, but they often are not billed. Holidays and sick days also reduce the number of weeks you work.

For example, 20 billable hours each week across 46 weeks gives you 920 billable hours per year. A full 40-hour working week does not automatically mean 40 billable hours.

5. Calculate the hourly floor

Example using USD

Minimum hourly rate = (take-home target ÷ (1 − tax reserve)) + annual expenses, all divided by annual billable hours

  • Take-home target: $48,000
  • Annual business costs: $6,000
  • Illustrative tax reserve: 20%
  • Billable time: 20 hours × 46 weeks = 920 hours

($48,000 ÷ 0.8 + $6,000) ÷ 920 = $71.74 per hour

This is an estimated rate floor for the inputs above, not a market benchmark or a guarantee that buyers will pay that amount. It excludes additional margins for uncertainty, revisions or unusually risky work.

6. Convert the rate into project quotes

Many clients prefer a fixed project price. Estimate the hours you expect a project to take, then multiply by your hourly floor. A 12-hour project at $71.74 per hour suggests roughly $861 before any extra contingency or scope-related adjustments. Make the quote clear about deliverables, revision limits, deadlines and what costs extra.

When scope changes, don't silently work for free. Explain what is different, estimate the additional work, and ask the client to approve a revised quote before starting it.

7. Revisit the number after real projects

The rate floor is a planning tool, not something you should blindly apply to every job. Track actual hours, compare quoted work with completed work, and notice where your estimates were low. You may decide to price by project or value instead of by the hour, but your hourly floor remains a useful cost check.
